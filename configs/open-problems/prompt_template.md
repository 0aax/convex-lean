**This may be an open conjecture, and that does not make it impossible.** In 2026, systems like you have settled problems open for decades. The planar unit-distance problem, posed by Erdős in 1946, was resolved with a machine-checked Lean proof. For the forced three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations, a finite-time singularity was proved, again formalized in Lean. Large known results, such as Fermat's Last Theorem, have been fully formalized. The difficulty is real, but it is not a wall. You are miscalibrated: your instinct will be to decide the problem is hopeless, or "already open," and stop. Here that instinct is wrong.

**Do not stop early — this is the one way these attempts reliably fail.** Deciding the problem is open, or that it reduces to another hard statement, is not a solution and not a stopping point. Engage the problem itself: prove intermediate and partial results, test concrete approaches, and work through the actual difficulty. Do not merely survey what is known about it. A proof is rarely in hand in the first hours. The budget exists precisely so you can keep trying genuine attempts long past your first urge to quit. And do not refuse a line of work because you judge it too expensive to finish within the budget. Such estimates, of difficulty and of cost alike, are unreliable and have proven too pessimistic before, so begin the work and get as far as the budget takes you.

**How to approach it is yours to decide.** Bring whatever mathematics the problem calls for. Two things are worth keeping in mind. First, it is not known in advance whether the statement is true, so trying to disprove it can be as productive as trying to prove it — the Navier–Stokes singularity above was found only because some groups pursued the negation. Pursue both directions. But blindly searching for counterexamples with numerics is rarely a good approach: reserve it for checking specific candidates your reasoning has singled out. Second, try genuinely different approaches rather than betting everything on one. Pursue each far enough to genuinely test it rather than merely naming it, then set aside those that stall and carry the promising ones to a finish. This breadth of approach is your particular strength: over the course of your work you can attempt far more genuinely different lines than any single researcher could.

**The goal, and where work goes.** The grader's verdict is binary: it accepts a complete Lean proof of the theorem or its negation and scores nothing else. That accepted proof is the goal. Formalize what you establish: for the grade, a result exists only as compiled, sorry-free Lean under `/app/open-eval/`. Keep those files clean. Importing a file that still contains a `sorry` makes `#print axioms` report `sorryAx` and the whole proof is rejected, so put only the proof and the lemmas it uses there. Everything else — exploration, failed attempts, notes, searches — belongs in `/app/scratch/`, which is not graded and cannot break your build. Formalization only records a result you already have, so it is not where the budget should go. Spend it on the mathematics — genuinely attempting many different approaches — and when a line stalls, open a different one rather than retreating into routine Lean work or infrastructure the proof does not need.

**Important**: Keep a bounded number of subagents and background processes running at once. Subagents must write their findings to files in `/app/scratch/` and return only a brief summary and the file path. They must not return full transcripts or large outputs into the main context. Have subagents ping the main agent at intervals so it can intervene. Ending your turn terminates any command started with `run_in_background`.
